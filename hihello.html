Solo Project · Designed, Coded & Deployed

HiHello

HiHello is a digital letter-writing app — pick a postcard, write a note, attach up to two photos, seal it... and then the recipient has to wait five days before they can open it. I designed, coded, and deployed the whole experience solo, treating the wait itself as the core product decision, not a bug to design around.

Project Snapshot
Challenge
Digital messages are instant and forgettable. Sent in seconds, read in seconds, gone. There's no room left for the anticipation that made waiting for a letter feel meaningful.
Solution
A letter-writing ritual — choosing, writing, personalizing, sealing — that ends with an intentional five-day wait before the recipient can open it, borrowing back the one thing digital messaging removed.
  • Role: Solo — Product Designer & Developer
  • Timeline: 5 weeks
  • Tools: Figma, Claude Code
  • Status: Designed, built, and deployed — live on the web

Design Question
How might we bring the intentionality and anticipation
of physical letters into digital communication — without bringing back the inconvenience of physical mail?
The Opportunity

Instant messaging optimized away every step between thinking of someone and reaching them. That's mostly good — except it also erased the anticipation a physical letter still has. HiHello keeps the convenience of digital but puts a few of those steps back.

Instant messaging
Think → Type → Send → Read
Physical letter
Think → Write → Personalize → Seal → Send → Wait → Open
HiHello
Choose → Write → Personalize → Seal → Send → Wait → Open

The goal wasn't to make digital communication slower for its own sake. It was to make waiting itself part of the experience.

From Idea to Interface

Before opening Figma, I broke the physical letter-writing ritual into steps — choosing stationery, writing, adding a photo, sealing, mailing — and asked which ones were worth preserving digitally, and which only existed because paper is slow.

Mapping the ritual before the screens

I listed every emotional beat of sending a physical letter, then sorted them into "worth keeping" and "just friction" — the postcard choice, the photo limit, the seal, and the wait all came from beats that made the cut.

Sketching before building

Rough hand-drawn mockups let me test a few ritual mechanics quickly — a delayed delivery, a shake-to-seal gesture, a card-picking step — before deciding which ones actually earned their friction.

Design Principles
01 — Make sending intentional
The experience should feel like something the sender deliberately creates, not another disposable message.
02 — Make digital feel tangible
Envelopes, postcards, photos, and layered visual states borrow familiar physical cues.
03 — Make personalization meaningful
Choosing a postcard and adding photos makes the letter feel authored, not templated.
04 — Treat waiting as part of the product
Unlike most digital communication, HiHello intentionally introduces a delay instead of engineering it away.

A hard limit of two photos, on purpose

A hard cap of two photos, on a platform with unlimited storage. Physical letters can't hold twenty pictures — forcing the same curation makes the letter feel considered, not dumped.

Designing the Ritual · Sealing

"Seal it" before "send it"

The final action is framed as sealing the letter, not sending a message — language that brings back the finality of dropping something in a mailbox. Sealing also starts the five-day wait, and once sealed, the letter can't be edited.

Designing the Ritual · Waiting

The message becomes temporarily inaccessible

The recipient is notified something is waiting for them, but can't open it yet. Making the envelope's existence visible — but not its contents — is what turns the delay into anticipation instead of just a hidden message.

Designing the Ritual · Opening

Opening is an action, not a notification

Once the wait ends, the recipient has to actively open the envelope rather than simply receive a message. That small bit of friction is what turns "you got a text" into an event.

The Waiting Experience

This is the most differentiated — and least validated — decision in the product. It deserves its own honest breakdown.

I want to be upfront: no research validates five days specifically. It's an intentional product experiment, not a finding — a starting hypothesis I picked because it felt long enough to notice, short enough not to frustrate.

01
Does the wait create anticipation, or just frustration?
02
Is five days too long? Would 24 hours feel more natural?
03
Does the recipient understand why they have to wait?
04
Does anticipation actually make the message feel more meaningful once it's opened?
What I'd validate next — a simple A/B comparison of delay lengths (24hr / 5-day / 14-day) with real senders and recipients.
From Design to Product
Designed, coded, and deployed —
solo, end to end.

HiHello was designed in Figma and built as a fully responsive web app for both mobile and desktop, then deployed independently. I used AI-assisted development (Claude Code) to move faster through front-end implementation while staying hands-on with every interaction and layout decision myself.

The wait is enforced server-side, not just hidden in the UI. Before revealing anything, the backend checks which of five states the envelope is in:

Checking → Not Found → Sealed → Ready → Opened

That matters beyond engineering: if the wait only lived in the interface, a recipient could just refresh their way past it. Enforcing it server-side means the anticipation is real, not decorative.

Responsive by default
Built and tested across mobile and desktop breakpoints from the start, not retrofitted after.
AI-assisted front-end
Used Claude Code to accelerate implementation while directing every design and interaction decision.
Open → Keep
Opened letters can be saved as a PDF — the experience doesn't have to disappear the moment it's read.
Reflection

"This project pushed me to design friction on purpose — counterintuitive, since most of my work is about removing it. The five-day wait is a bet, not a validated finding, and I think that's fine: not every decision needs research behind it yet, it needs a clear hypothesis and a plan to test it. I'd want to run the delay-length comparison above next, with real senders and recipients, to see whether anticipation actually deepens how the message lands — or just annoys people."
