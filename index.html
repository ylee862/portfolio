Student Well-being App

A research-driven behavioral awareness experience designed to help students recognize emotional patterns, contextual triggers, and stress responses over time.

Figma Usability Testing UX Research Think-aloud Testing Pre/Post Surveys Affinity Mapping Personas Journey Mapping

I wanted to explore why wellness apps often fail to create meaningful behavioral change. Most tools help users record emotions, but very few help them understand the context behind them. This project explored how behavioral awareness could be designed directly into the product experience — helping students recognize emotional triggers, reflect on patterns over time, and build healthier stress responses through guided intervention and self-awareness.

Behavioral patterns shaped the information architecture

Research revealed students needed contextual reflection rather than isolated metrics. Instead of prioritizing dashboards, the experience was structured around connecting emotional states, routines, and sleep quality into a single awareness loop. The IA wasn't designed from convention — it was designed from observed behavior.

Designing for reflection instead of tracking

The interface intentionally de-emphasized analytics and increased focus on behavioral interpretation through guided reflection moments. Instead of a dashboard of numbers, users were met with questions: "What was different about Tuesday?" These weren't prompts — they were the product.

Core Insight
The barrier wasn't
functionality.
It was vocabulary.

Users didn't know terms like "Dutch angle" or "Rembrandt lighting," so they couldn't access features they technically already had. The tool wasn't broken — the language was.

Users couldn't describe what they couldn't visualize

The interface depended on users already understanding photography language. This created an accessibility gap where beginners couldn't explore features they didn't know how to name. The problem wasn't capability — it was translation. Users had a visual idea in their head but no vocabulary bridge to the interface.

Replacing terminology with visual recognition

Instead of typing expert terminology, users select visual examples directly. The interaction shifts from recall-based input — "remember the right word" — to recognition-based exploration: "pick what looks right." The cognitive demand drops from expert memory to beginner intuition. The feature set stays the same. The barrier disappears.

HCI Project · Gamification

Wordtropolis

A gamified spelling learning platform designed for young students — built around how children actually learn, stay motivated, and retain information over time.

Figma Java Swing Gamification Child HCI Adaptive Learning Multi-sensory Design

Existing spelling tools were boring by design — and that's a design failure. Children were learning despite the interface, not because of it. I wanted to close that gap: build a system where the learning IS the game mechanic, not content dressed up in a game shell.

The Problem
Spelling tools are boring
by design.
That's a design failure.

Existing tools suffered from low engagement, static interaction, and unclear feedback loops. Children were learning in spite of the interface — not because of it. The root issue: these tools were built around content delivery, not around the psychology of how kids actually stay motivated and retain information.

Problem 01
Low engagement — tools prioritized content delivery over motivation and reward loops
Problem 02
Static, repetitive interaction — no sense of progress, narrative, or meaningful challenge
Problem 03
Feedback was unclear or delayed — children didn't know why they were right or wrong
Research → Design
📚
📸 Research grounding — annotated competitor analysis or engagement framework
Show what existing tools got wrong — annotate their failure points
Existing tool analysis — engagement failures annotated

Learning science grounded every design decision

The redesign wasn't based on intuition about what kids might like. It was grounded in three principles from learning science: intrinsic motivation (children engage more when challenge feels meaningful), spaced repetition (returning to difficult words at optimal intervals improves retention), and multi-sensory reinforcement (visual + audio feedback accelerates learning). Each design decision traces back to one of these principles.

The game arc wasn't decoration — it was the structure

Instead of wrapping content in a game skin, the mission → boss battle progression IS the information architecture. Each mission is a spaced repetition session. The boss battle is the mastery check. The narrative arc gives children a reason to return — and a real sense of accomplishment when they reach the end. The progression system was designed before the visual layer.

Key Design Decisions
🎯
Missions → boss battle arc
Gave children a meaningful destination and a sense of narrative momentum — not just a streak counter.
📈
Adaptive difficulty
The system adjusted challenge based on performance — spaced repetition baked into the game loop, not bolted on top.
✨
Multi-sensory feedback
Visual celebration + audio cues on correct responses reinforce learning and maintain engagement for young users.
