Fitoday

A niche social app for fashion enthusiasts to share one outfit a day, get structured community feedback, and track their style progression — designed and being built solo, end to end.

onboarding flow
Project Snapshot
  • Role: Solo — Product Designer, UX Researcher & Full-Stack Developer
  • Timeline: 8–10 weeks, ongoing
  • Platform: iOS (Flutter)
  • Stack: Flutter/Dart, Firebase (Auth, Firestore, Cloud Functions)
  • Status: Research, PRD, and Figma design complete — auth & backend security completed, core Cloud Functions in progress
The Spark
It didn't start with a market gap.
It started with scrolling Instagram, wishing someone would just tell me what was wrong with my outfit.

My thought: How can one dress that well? I want to get honest feedbacks on my outfit so I can dress well too. I need small judement calls: the colour combination, the length, the proportions, the vibe.

That was a personal itch, not a product idea. What turned it into one was a simple question: was I really the only person who felt this way? That question is what sent me into research instead of straight into Figma.

Core Insight
People weren't short on places
to post an outfit. They were short on a place to actually get feedback on it.

To find out if others felt the same way, I looked at where fashion content already lives — Instagram, Pinterest, Lemon8 — and read through user reviews of each. The pattern was consistent: some didn't trust these platforms anymore, whether because of ad density, opaque algorithms, or moderation that feels arbitrary.

Along with that, none of them were built for a specific, recurring loop of "post my outfit, get real feedback, see myself improve."

Finding 01
People want honest feedback on their outfit but are afraid of public criticism — a small, 12-person survey (student/fashion-enthusiast sample) surfaced this consistently
Finding 02
Fashion inspiration is saved everywhere — Pinterest, Instagram, TikTok — with no centralized memory of it
Finding 03

A note on research scope

The competitive review analysis and the 12-person survey were both informal and self-run research. It was not a statistically representative study. I'm treating the findings above as directional signal that shaped the product, not as validated market data.

Research → Requirements
Once the gap was confirmed,
I went straight into writing the requirements.

Rather than sketching screens first, I wrote a full product requirements document directly from the research: onboarding & style personalization, posting constraints, a dual-tab search engine, community rating & anonymized voter feedback, a gamified leveling system, and social/wishlist features — plus non-functional requirements around load times and the "midnight crunch" write-load spike when every user's daily score finalizes at once. Writing the PRD myself, rather than jumping straight to UI, is what let every later design decision trace back to a specific finding instead of a gut feeling.

Critical Design Decisions

Three constraints carry almost the entire product. Each one was a deliberate trade-off, not a default.

One post per day, not unlimited
Unlimited posting was the obvious default, but it leads to spam and a noisy feed. A strict daily limit creates scarcity, forces more thoughtful posting, and gives the habit loop somewhere to attach itself.
Three-option rating, not likes
A like is a binary signal that tells you nothing about how to improve. "Could be Improved / It's Okay / It's Awesome" forces a real judgment while staying lightweight enough to answer in one tap.
Show who voted, hide what they voted
Fully public ratings discourage honest feedback and create social pressure. Fully anonymous voting removes accountability. Showing the voter list but hiding the individual rating splits the difference.
Validation & Iteration

I ran a 5-person usability test on the Figma prototype, asking participants to post an outfit, save an item, and use the rating system — recording task success, completion time, and where people got stuck.

01
The three-option rating system tested well — all five participants understood it immediately with no explanation needed
02
Several participants wanted a way to explain why they gave a rating, not just which of the three options they picked

"but I want people to tell me WHERE to improve not just how good it looks"
03
One participant didn't want their historical scores visible to others at all — surfacing a privacy need the original scope hadn't accounted for
04
A participant who mis-tapped submit during testing wanted to correct their vote — surfacing a need for vote modification within the rating window

What changed between V1 and V2

Three changes came directly out of that test: an optional comment field after rating; a per-user toggle for whether rated outfits show on a public profile or stay private to the owner; and the ability to change a vote within the 24-hour rating window instead of it being final on submit.

Student Well-being App

A student well-being platform that helps users identify emotional patterns, strengthen personal growth competencies, and build healthier coping habits through guided reflection and behavioral tracking.

Figma Usability Testing UX Research Think-aloud Testing Pre/Post Surveys Affinity Mapping Personas Journey Mapping
Project Snapshot
  • Role: Lead UX Researcher & Designer & Intern
  • Timeline: 17 weeks, Summer Internship 2025
  • Team: Worked with a team including education faculty and senior developers
  • Tools: Figma, usability testing protocol, affinity mapping
  • Status: Research & design complete — backend/chatbot development ongoing, final UI confidential
Core Insight
Students didn't need more ways
to record how they felt.
They needed help understanding why.

Through literature review, we found that emotion regulation sits at the core of student well-being. Yet most wellness apps were built around recording emotions alonee without helping users understand what triggered them or how other competencies affected their well-being. The research pointed clearly in one direction: students gained more from contextual reflection and guided interpretation than from tracking alone.

Pre-survey responses
Finding 01
Students could identify stress in hindsight, but struggled to recognize patterns while experiencing them
Finding 02
Emotional triggers became easier to recognize when paired with contextual reflection instead of isolated tracking
Finding 03
Short-term awareness and guided intervention created opportunities for healthier long-term stress responses

An alternative we considered and rejected

Our first direction was a sleep-focused app that tracked university students' sleep schedules as a lever for emotion regulation. After completing the literature review, we found sleep alone was too narrow — regulation depended on a broader set of personal competencies, like resilience and social support, not rest in isolation. That finding is what moved us away from sleep-tracking and toward the competency-based framework that shaped the rest of this project.

Research → Design
Persona — Jenna Andrews. Her habit of noticing stress only in hindsight is what pushed the design toward contextual, in-the-moment reflection instead of passive logging.

Context became more important than metrics

Research findings shaped the product's structure at every level. Rather than centering the experience on passive logging, the platform guided users through recurring reflection loops tied to real-life contexts — workload, social interaction, routines, sleep, and stress. This helped users connect emotional states to specific behavioural patterns instead of treating emotions as isolated data points.

Designing for long-term engagement

One major challenge in wellness products is sustaining meaningful engagement after onboarding. To address this, the experience introduced a competency-based growth system where users could choose emotional competencies they wanted to strengthen (such as resilience, emotion regulation, or social support) and work toward targeting those areas through monthly reflections, guided challenges, and personal goals. Completing these activities built their competency level visibly over time, giving users a clear sense of direction and progress rather than just a log of past moods.

Competency-based progression flow — users choose emotional competencies to strengthen and receive structured goals, challenges, and reflection pathways to support long-term growth. This flow is the direct output of the sleep-vs-competency trade-off above, and became the "Competency-based progression" decision below.
Key Design Decisions
Reflection-first interaction
Shifted the experience away from passive tracking toward active behavioural interpretation and emotional awareness.
Competency-based progression
Users could identify which emotional competencies they wanted to strengthen and receive structured goals, challenges, and reflection pathways to support long-term growth.
Cognitive simplicity
Reduced interface complexity to keep focus on self-awareness rather than overwhelming users with analytics.
Testing & Iteration

We ran usability testing with 18 Western University students using a three-part protocol: a pre-survey to establish baseline emotional awareness habits, a think-aloud interactive session with the V1 prototype, and a post-survey to capture immediate perception shifts. The V2 prototype was iterated directly from the patterns and pain points surfaced in those sessions.

Testing analysis — synthesis of pre/post survey data and think-aloud sessions across 18 participants. This synthesis is what directly surfaced Finding 01 (below) and led to the V2 onboarding redesign.
"I actually stopped to think about why I felt that way — not just that I did."
01
Most participants arrived expecting a standard mood tracker but the reflection-first structure required a brief mindset shift, which the V2 onboarding was redesigned to address
02
The social goal-sharing feature was consistently flagged as a strong motivation to return. The participants said it made the app feel less like a solo task
03
Post-survey responses showed increased self-reported emotional clarity compared to pre-survey baselines, suggesting the reflection structure had an immediate effect even within a single session

Reflection

"This project changed how I think about behavioral UX. Designing for well-being isn't about collecting more information. It's about helping users recognize themselves within it. It also deepened my interest in how design choices can shape behaviour over time: how the structure of an experience, not just its visuals, can gradually shift how someone understands and relates to themselves."

Nanobanana Feature Enhancement

Reducing cognitive friction in AI-assisted photo editing through visual interaction design after identifying the accessibility gap through community research and proposing a concrete interaction redesign to address it.

Interaction Design Community Research Visual UI UX Strategy Cognitive Load Reduction
Project Snapshot
  • Role: Solo — UX Researcher & Interaction Designer
  • Timeline: 8 weeks
  • Type: Self-initiated case study
  • Tools: Community research (Reddit, user reviews), Figma
Core Insight
The barrier wasn't
functionality.
It was vocabulary.

Most users don't think in photography or videography terminology — they think in feelings and visuals. When the interface required them to translate a creative idea into the right technical word, many simply gave up before they started. The tool's capabilities were never in question; the gap was in how users were expected to express what they wanted.

Community research — annotated evidence from Reddit and user reviews. View full research database
Insight 01
Users were dissatisfied with their results because the terminology required to get what they wanted was unfamiliar. They asked for the wrong thing, not knowing the right words existed
Insight 02
Creative exploration depended entirely on prior photography or videography knowledge. Users had a visual idea in mind but couldn't convert it into a prompt the tool understood
Insight 03
Text prompts assumed expertise from the start. The only guidance offered was a basic template ("create me an image about [topic]"), with no visual reference or vocabulary support for beginners
Research → Design

Users couldn't describe what they visualize

Community research — annotated pain points

Replacing terminology with visual recognition

Instead of typing expert terminology, users select visual examples directly. The interaction shifts from recall-based input — "remember the right word" — to recognition-based exploration: "pick what looks right." The cognitive demand drops from expert memory to beginner intuition. The feature set stays the same and the barrier disappears.


Interaction model shift — from recall to recognition
Key Design Decisions
Recognition over recall
Users choose by seeing instead of remembering technical terminology. The interface does the translation.
Exploration without expertise
Removed the assumption that users already understood creative language.
Visual-first onboarding
Lowered intimidation and encouraged experimentation through immediate, consequence-free visual feedback.
Step 1: New experience — image grid appears with lighting/angle/composition thumbnails, directly replacing the free-text prompt that caused Insight 01 and 03
Step 2: User taps a thumbnail → immediate style preview updates, so users recognize the right choice (Insight 02) instead of having to name it
Step 3: Final output with selected style applied
Early Validation

Before treating recognition-over-recall as settled, I ran a small informal test with 3 people who had no photography or videography background. I first asked each person to describe, in words, a scene they had in mind. Then I asked them to rebuild the same scene by picking preview images for angle, lighting, and proportion one at a time. Across all three, the image built through visual selection was noticeably closer to what they had originally pictured than what they were able to produce from a written description alone.

This was a small, informal test. Not a formal usability study with a real sample size. However, it's a genuine early signal that recognition-based selection outperforms recall for non-expert users on this task.

Intended Outcomes

This is a proposed redesign, not a shipped or fully user-tested feature — the concept had an informal validation pass (see Early Validation above), but the points below still describe design intent, not measured production results.

Designed to
  • Reduce cognitive barriers for beginner users during onboarding. It means no prior creative knowledge required
  • Increase accessibility of advanced creative features without reducing capability
  • Encourage exploration by letting users discover options through visual browsing rather than recall
  • Reframe prompting as recognition instead of recall, which is a shift in the interaction model itself
Reflection

"This project reinforced how often UX friction comes from assumptions embedded in language. Sometimes accessibility is not about simplifying features — it's about simplifying interpretation."

Gamification · Learning Experience Design · Child HCI

Wordtropolis

A gamified spelling learning platform designed for young students — built around motivation psychology, adaptive challenge, and meaningful progression. The project ran under a tight academic timeline, which pushed us to prioritize decisions quickly and ground every choice in learning science rather than intuition.

Figma Java Swing Gamification Child HCI Adaptive Learning Multi-sensory Design
Project Snapshot
  • Role: Main UI design + Java Swing implementation, within a team
  • Timeline: 12 weeks, university course project
  • Team: Course team project
  • Tools: Figma, Java Swing
The Problem
The original spelling tool was boring
by design.
That's a design failure.

The original spelling tool suffered from low engagement, static interaction, and unclear feedback loops. The root issue: this tool was built around content delivery, not around the psychology of how kids actually stay motivated and retain information.

The original spelling game interface
Problem 01
Low engagement — tools prioritized content delivery over motivation and reward loops
Problem 02
Static, repetitive interaction — no sense of progress, narrative, or meaningful challenge
Problem 03
Feedback was unclear or delayed — children didn't know their next action or whether the answer was right or wrong
Research → Design

Learning science grounded every design decision

The redesign wasn't based on intuition about what kids might like. It was grounded in three principles from learning science:

- intrinsic motivation (children engage more when challenge feels meaningful)
- spaced repetition (returning to difficult words at optimal intervals improves retention)
- multi-sensory reinforcement (visual + audio feedback accelerates learning)

Each design decision traces back to one of these principles.


The game arc structure

Rather than layering a game skin on top of existing content, I focused, as UI lead, on the mission → boss battle progression structure. Each mission functions as a spaced repetition session, returning to difficult words at intervals optimized for retention. The boss battle serves as the mastery check — a culminating challenge that gives the progression real stakes. The narrative arc gives children a genuine reason to return, and a real sense of accomplishment when they reach the end. Crucially, the progression system was designed before any visual decisions were made.

Game arc — mission flow showing how levels lead to boss battle
Key Design Decisions
Mission-based progression
Structured learning around visible goals and meaningful milestones to sustain engagement over time.
Adaptive challenge system
Difficulty adjusted dynamically based on performance, balancing frustration and accomplishment.
Multi-sensory feedback
Visual celebration + audio cues on correct responses reinforce learning and maintain engagement for young users.
Design → Build
This wasn't just a concept.
It was a fully realized interactive prototype.

The full experience was drafted in Figma and implemented in Java Swing, allowing gameplay pacing, adaptive challenge, and feedback systems to be tested beyond static mockups.

Designing through implementation

Implementing the experience exposed how gameplay pacing, feedback timing, and adaptive challenge directly affected user engagement in practice. Building the prototype end-to-end strengthened the relationship between interaction decisions and behavioural outcomes.


Draft -> Java Swing
Final Outcome
Mission screen — spelling challenge tied to the cat character, part of the spaced-repetition mission loop
Narrative obstacle screen — gives the mission stakes beyond a plain word list
Progress-gated screen — visualizes the adaptive-challenge system blocking advancement until mastery
Boss battle — the mastery check that closes out each mission arc
Outcome
  • Reimagined a traditional spelling game using behavioural and child-centered UX principles
  • Explored how progression systems can support educational engagement and retention
  • Built a fully interactive prototype implementing adaptive challenge and multi-sensory feedback
  • Explored how gameplay structure can become the foundation of a learning experience rather than a decorative layer
  • Evaluated by course TAs and the professor, who noted it stood out from other teams' submissions — though it was not tested directly with children (see Reflection)
Reflection

"Working within a tight school project timeline pushed me to prioritize decisions quickly and defend them clearly. Given more time, I would have explored more advanced adaptive features and conducted user testing with children directly. But the core lesson stuck: effective learning experiences aren't built by adding rewards on top of education — they emerge when motivation itself becomes part of the interaction system."
